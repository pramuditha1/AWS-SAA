IAM — who can do what, and for how long

Must-knowDomain 1 · Tasks 1.1–1.2 · ~30% of the exam

IAM is AWS’s identity system. It answers two questions: who are you, and what may you do? On the exam you pick the right identity (user, role, or federation), the right kind of policy, and the smallest blast radius that still gets the job done.

Start here — the W questions

Why

Cloud accounts hold real data and real money. You need a lock on every API call: only the right person or app, only the actions they need, only for as long as they need them.

What

IAM (Identity and Access Management) is a free, global AWS service for authentication (prove who you are) and authorization (decide what you may do). You create identities and attach policies — JSON documents that allow or deny actions on resources.

Where

IAM is global. A user or role you create is available in every Region. The resources those identities call (an S3 bucket, a KMS key) are often Regional — the identity is still the same.

Which pieces matter

Root user · IAM user / group / role · identity vs resource policy · trust policy · permission boundary · Organizations SCP · STS temporary credentials. Those ideas cover almost every SAA IAM question.

Core knowledge (beginner foundations)

Start with the root user — then stop using it

Every AWS account begins with a root user: the email and password that can do everything, including recover the account. AWS tells you not to use root for daily work. Enable MFA on root, do not create root access keys, and do everyday admin with IAM identities instead. If a stem says “everyday administration” and an answer still uses root, that answer is wrong.

Principal — the “who” making a request: a user, a role session, a federated identity, or (sometimes) another AWS service.

Policy — a document of Allow and Deny statements. Policies attach to identities, or they live on resources (bucket policy, key policy), or they sit at the organization as guardrails.

STS (Security Token Service) — the API that hands out temporary credentials when you assume a role. Temporary means they expire. That is why roles beat long-lived access keys on the exam.

How IAM decides allow or deny (from AWS docs)

A principal sends a request: identity, action, resource, and optional conditions (source IP, MFA, time, tags). IAM evaluates every applicable policy. AWS’s evaluation logic is the story the exam expects you to reason about.

flowchart TD req[API_request] --> deny{{Any_explicit_Deny}} deny -->|yes| stop[Denied] deny -->|no| scp{{SCP_allows_if_in_Org}} scp -->|no| stop scp -->|yes| bound{{Permission_boundary_allows}} bound -->|no| stop bound -->|yes| allow{{Identity_or_resource_Allow}} allow -->|yes| ok[Allowed] allow -->|no| impl[Implicit_deny]

Follows IAM policy evaluation logic. Open that page for AWS’s official diagram and the full decision tree.

  1. Explicit deny anywhere (identity policy, resource policy, SCP, session policy, permission boundary) — request denied. This always wins, even over AdministratorAccess.
  2. Organizations SCP — if the account is in an organization, the SCP must allow the action or evaluation stops. SCPs never grant access by themselves.
  3. Permission boundary — if one is attached, it caps the maximum permissions. The identity policy cannot exceed it.
  4. Identity-based allow or resource-based allow — at least one allow is required.
  5. Otherwise implicit deny. New IAM users start with no permissions until you attach a policy. That is the default, not a bug.

Identities — pick the right principal

The exam’s favorite correct answer for apps and cross-account access is a role: temporary credentials via STS, no standing secret on disk.

Policy types (explained simply)

Identity-based policies attach to a user, group, or role. They say “this identity may call these actions on these resources.” Prefer customer-managed policies you can reuse and edit. Inline policies stick to one identity.

Resource-based policies live on the resource (S3 bucket policy, KMS key policy, SQS queue policy, Lambda resource policy). They are how you grant another account access without creating a user in your account. Not every service supports them; when one does not, use a role whose trust policy names the other account.

Permission boundaries do not grant anything. They cap what an identity can ever receive. Classic story: let a developer create roles for their app, but force every new role to carry a boundary so they cannot escalate to AdministratorAccess.

Service control policies (SCPs) attach to the organization root, OUs, or member accounts. They are account-wide guardrails (“no one in this OU may disable CloudTrail”). SCPs do not apply to the management account. A member admin with AdministratorAccess still cannot do what the SCP denies.

Session policies shrink a role further for one STS session. Think of them as a temporary filter on top of the role.

Trust policy vs permissions policy

Every role has two documents. The trust policy answers “who may call sts:AssumeRole?” The permissions policy answers “once assumed, what may they do?” Exam distractors swap these. If Account B cannot assume a role in Account A, the defect is almost always the trust policy in A — not the S3 permissions on the role.

For EC2 the trust principal is ec2.amazonaws.com. For Lambda, lambda.amazonaws.com. For a user in Account B, the principal is that user’s or account’s ARN. Get the trust wrong and STS returns AccessDenied even if permissions are *:*.

Use case 1 — app on EC2 reads S3

Need: Photo-processing instances read from incoming/ and write to processed/. No long-term keys. No delete.

Design: Instance profile role with a customer-managed policy: s3:GetObject on the incoming prefix, s3:PutObject on the processed prefix. Not AmazonS3FullAccess.

Use case 2 — cross-account without duplicating users

Need: Analytics engineers in Account Analytics query a data-lake bucket in Account Lake. Lake will not create IAM users for them.

Design: Role in Lake that trusts Analytics (or specific role ARNs) and allows list/get on the bucket. Grant engineers sts:AssumeRole in Analytics. Optional ExternalId for third-party assume-role.

Use case 3 — company-wide logging nobody can turn off

Need: Forty accounts in Organizations. A sandbox admin must not be able to stop CloudTrail, even with AdministratorAccess.

Design: SCP on the OU (and org) that denies stop/delete trail actions. Enable CloudTrail from the management or delegated admin account. Identity policies in the member account can be removed by a local admin; SCPs cannot.

Case study 1 — least privilege on compute

Stem

A photo-processing fleet on EC2 must read objects from incoming/ and write results to processed/ in the same bucket. Security wants no long-term keys on the instances, and instances must not delete objects. Which design meets the requirement?

A — wrong Create an IAM user, put access keys in user data, attach AmazonS3FullAccess.
B — wrong Attach AmazonS3FullAccess to an instance profile role.
C — correct Instance profile role with a customer-managed policy: s3:GetObject on …/incoming/* and s3:PutObject on …/processed/*.
D — wrong Make the bucket public and use anonymous HTTPS.

A stores long-term keys. B uses a role (good) but FullAccess can delete — it fails least privilege. D is a data leak. C is the documented pattern: role, narrow ARNs, narrow actions.

Case study 2 — cross-account without duplicating users

Stem

Analytics in Account Analytics must run Athena over a data-lake bucket in Account Lake. Lake will not create IAM users for the analytics engineers. Engineers already have IAM users in Analytics. The solution must be auditable in CloudTrail in both accounts.

A — wrong Share the Lake root password with the analytics lead.
B — correct Role in Lake that trusts Analytics and allows s3:GetObject / s3:ListBucket. Grant engineers sts:AssumeRole. Optionally add ExternalId.
C — wrong Nightly aws s3 sync into Analytics with a stored access key.
D — wrong Disable Block Public Access and gate by a long random prefix.

B uses AssumeRole so both accounts see the session in CloudTrail. C duplicates data and reintroduces keys. D is not authentication.

Case study 3 — guardrails a member admin cannot remove

Stem

Forty accounts in AWS Organizations. A developer in a sandbox attached AdministratorAccess and turned off CloudTrail. Leadership wants every account, including future ones, unable to stop logging — even if someone is a local administrator.

A — wrong Document “do not disable CloudTrail” in a wiki.
B — wrong Attach a deny for cloudtrail:StopLogging to each IAM user in every account.
C — correct SCP on the sandbox OU (and org) denying stop/delete trail actions; enable CloudTrail from management or delegated admin.
D — wrong Permission boundary on the one developer who caused the incident.

Identity policies in a member account can be removed by that account’s admin. SCPs cannot. B misses new users and roles. D is too narrow.

Tricky points (still explained simply)

Explicit deny always wins. One Deny in any applicable policy beats every Allow.

Groups cannot contain groups. Need hierarchy across accounts? That is Organizations OUs — not nested IAM groups.

Roles do not have access keys. If the question is “rotate credentials for an app on EC2,” move to a role — do not create a second access key.

KMS is special. For KMS, the key policy must allow the caller; an identity policy alone is not enough. See the KMS / security chapter.

MFA Delete on S3 versioning is enabled by the root user, not by a normal IAM admin. One of the few remaining root-only tasks.

Three different “find unused” tools: Access Analyzer (resources shared outside the account), credential report (users and key ages), Access Advisor (last-used services for right-sizing). Do not mix them.

IAM is eventually consistent. A brand-new role can briefly fail AssumeRole. More often the intended answer is a bad trust policy or missing sts:AssumeRole.

Exam habit When two answers both “work,” keep the one that (1) uses a role instead of keys, (2) names the smallest resource ARN, (3) cannot be undone by a member-account admin if the stem asked for a company-wide control.
Light touch on the exam Exact JSON syntax, GetFederationToken vs GetSessionToken API details, IAM Identity Center console clicks. Know “temporary credentials via STS / a role”; do not deep-dive the STS API catalog.
