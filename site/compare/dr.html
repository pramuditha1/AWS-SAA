Disaster recovery picker — RTO, RPO, then money

High-score pageDomain 2.2 · Reliability · DR whitepaper

Disaster recovery questions give you two numbers and a budget. RTO is how long you may be down. RPO is how much data you may lose. Pick the cheapest strategy that still meets both. Also refuse “enable Multi-AZ” when the stem lost a whole Region — Multi-AZ is high availability inside one Region; Multi-Region is disaster recovery.

Start here — the W questions

Why

Hardware fails, AZs fail, and sometimes a whole Region fails. Backups, warm copies, and active-active stacks cost different amounts. Without RTO/RPO you cannot tell which design is “enough” versus “overkill.”

What

AWS names four strategies in the DR whitepaper: backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, and multi-site active/active. This picker matches RTO/RPO (and budget) to one of those four — and checks AZ vs Region blast radius.

Where

Multi-AZ stays inside one Region (HA). DR usually means a second Region: snapshots and AMIs, cross-Region replicas, S3 CRR, Aurora Global, DynamoDB Global Tables, plus Route 53 or Global Accelerator to cut traffic over.

Which pieces matter

RTO · RPO · the four strategies · Multi-AZ ≠ Multi-Region · backups even when you replicate · Route 53 failover vs latency · Aurora Global / DynamoDB Global Tables. Those ideas cover almost every DR stem.

Core knowledge — how to walk a stem

Blast radius, then numbers, then cost

Read once: did they lose an AZ or a Region? AZ → Multi-AZ HA (ALB across AZs, RDS Multi-AZ, Regional S3). Region → one of the four DR strategies. Then match RTO/RPO. Finally pick the cheapest strategy that still clears both numbers.

RPO is a data question: backup frequency, replication lag, or continuous replication. RTO is a compute-and-DNS question: how long to stand up infrastructure, restore, and cut Route 53. Replication without backups is not enough for ransomware or bad deploys — replication copies corruption, so you still keep point-in-time backups in the recovery Region.

The four strategies (plain English).

Backup and restore — backups, AMIs, and IaC live in the recovery Region. On disaster you deploy, restore, point DNS. Typical RTO: hours. Cheapest. Illegal when they want tens of minutes.

Pilot light — core data is always on in the recovery Region (RDS cross-Region replica or Aurora Global secondary, S3 CRR, DynamoDB). App servers are tiny or off until you scale them out. Typical RTO: tens of minutes. Low bill until disaster. Usual exam answer for “low RPO, moderate RTO, small bill.”

Warm standby — a scaled-down but fully functional stack is already running. You scale out, not build from zero. Typical RTO: minutes. Costs more than pilot light. Full-size passive copy is “hot standby” — you pay active-active money for active/passive.

Multi-site active/active — both Regions serve users (Route 53 latency or Global Accelerator). Near-zero RTO from the user’s view. Most expensive. Use when users on two continents must not wait for failover.

Case study 1 — five-minute RPO, thirty-minute RTO, small bill

Stem

RPO is 5 minutes. RTO is about 30 minutes. The secondary Region must not run a full-size fleet until a disaster. Budget is limited. The database is RDS PostgreSQL. Static assets are in S3.

A — wrong Nightly snapshots copied to the second Region. RPO is hours, not 5 minutes.
B — correct Pilot light: RDS cross-Region read replica (or Aurora Global if they moved), S3 CRR, AMIs replicated, app Auto Scaling at zero or tiny until failover, Route 53 failover.
C — wrong Warm standby at 100% capacity. Meets RTO, violates budget — that is hot standby / full-price active-passive.
D — wrong RDS Multi-AZ only. Protects an AZ, not a Region.

30 minutes is enough to scale compute if the data is already there. That is pilot light. Warm standby also meets the numbers but fails the budget / “no full-size fleet” constraint.

Case study 2 — Region is fine, one AZ died

Stem

An e-commerce site runs EC2 behind an ALB and RDS in a single Availability Zone. Last month that AZ had a power event and the site was down for two hours. Leadership wants the next AZ fault to be almost invisible. They are not required to survive an entire Region outage this year.

A — correct Multi-AZ HA: ASG across at least two AZs behind the ALB, and RDS Multi-AZ. Regional S3 classes already span AZs. Matches the failure they actually had.
B — wrong Active-active in two Regions with DynamoDB Global Tables. Bigger problem, possible rewrite, DR money on an HA requirement.
C — wrong Backup and restore to another Region. Hours of RTO is what they already suffered. Wrong failure domain.
D — wrong A single read replica in the same AZ. Same AZ, async, not HA for writes.

Read the blast radius. AZ → Multi-AZ. Region → one of the four DR strategies. Do not jump to Global Tables because they sound impressive.

Case study 3 — near-zero downtime, two continents

Stem

Users in Europe and the US must always hit a local healthy stack. RTO for a Region loss is near zero. The data model is key-value. Occasional cross-Region conflict is acceptable if last-writer wins. Cost is secondary.

A — wrong Pilot light in the US with all EU users on Frankfurt until failover. EU RTO is not near zero; EU latency is wrong on a normal day.
B — correct Multi-site active/active: DynamoDB Global Tables, app stacks in both Regions, Route 53 latency routing (or Global Accelerator) with health checks.
C — wrong RDS Multi-AZ in one Region. Wrong API and wrong failure domain.
D — wrong S3 CRR only, with compute in one Region. Objects are safe; the API is still one-Region.

Active/active needs an engine that can take writes in two Regions (or a clear primary). DynamoDB Global Tables match key-value + last-writer-wins. Aurora Global is the relational version with one primary writer (secondary can be promoted).

Tricky points

Meet RTO and RPO first, then minimize cost. The cheapest architecture that misses RPO is still wrong.

Replication copies deletions and ransomware. Keep point-in-time backups in the DR Region even if you replicate. S3 Object Lock / Vault Lock appears for ransomware immutability — not as a Region-failover substitute.

Route 53 failover vs latency vs weighted. Failover = active/passive health-check. Latency = active/active nearest Region. Weighted = gradual or blue/green. Prefer alias to ALB/CloudFront.

DMS with CDC is migration / cutover, not a DR strategy by itself. Elastic Disaster Recovery (DRS) is block-level continuous replication of servers — know the name; the four whitepaper strategies are the main answers.

Aurora Global published targets (typical RPO < 1 second, RTO < 1 minute) beat a hand-rolled replica when the engine can be Aurora. Treat them as design targets AWS publishes, not absolute guarantees.
